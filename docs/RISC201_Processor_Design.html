Read the spec again — it changes the answer

This is not an RTL project. Items 2–4 make it a software simulator project: an assembler, a disassembler, a CLI debugger, a microcode engine and an ALU class. That single fact reshapes how you should approach Part 1.

In hardware, "compare the behavior of the 4-stage and 6-stage" is answered by synthesis: you get an Fmax and an area number from the tools. In a simulator you get neither for free. A cycle-accurate model will happily tell you the 6-stage core takes more cycles — and if you stop there you will conclude, wrongly, that deeper is worse. The whole reason anyone lengthens a pipeline is to shorten the clock period, and a simulator does not model clock period unless you make it.

So the load-bearing decision in this project is one the spec never mentions: you must adopt an explicit delay model, state its assumptions, and report time-per-instruction, not just cycles. Everything in §01 is built around that. Do this and your evaluation section writes itself; skip it and item 5 collapses into a stall-count table with no conclusion in it.

Confirm before you build

The handout says "RISC201 ISA" but does not attach a spec. Everything below assumes RISC201 is the course's version of SimpleRisc from Sarangi — 21 instructions, 16 registers (r14=sp, r15=ra), fixed 32-bit encoding, three formats, a hidden 2-bit flags register. Get the exact opcode map from your instructor or handout and diff it against Sarangi ch. 3 before you write the encoder. An opcode table baked into the wrong assumption is expensive to unpick later.

The one architectural decision that makes the rest easy

You will write an assembler, a disassembler, three or four execution cores, a microcode assembler and a test suite. Each of those needs to know the same thing: what the instructions are and how they are encoded. Write that down once, as data, and generate everything else from it.

# isa/table.py — the single source of truth

  Inst("add",  0b00000, "R/I", ("rd","rs1","rs2|imm"), False, True),

  Inst("ld",   0b01110, "I",   ("rd","imm[rs1]"),      False, False),

The assembler's encoder, the disassembler's decoder, every core's decode stage, the microcode dispatch ROM and the random-program generator in your tests all read this list. When the opcode map turns out to be different from what you assumed, you change one row. This is worth an hour on day one and saves days later.

01a

What a 4-stage and a 6-stage core actually are

Sarangi's reference design is a 5-stage pipeline: IF (fetch) · OF (operand fetch: decode + register read) · EX (execute) · MA (memory access) · RW (register write). A 4-stage core merges two adjacent stages; a 6-stage core splits one. Which two you merge, and which one you split, is the entire design question — and it is not arbitrary.

Two rules that decide everything

Before looking at any specific organization, internalize these. They come straight from the structure of the pipeline and they let you predict the CPI of any depth without simulating it.

Rule 1 · control hazards

A branch costs one flushed instruction for every stage before the stage where its outcome and target become known. Stages added after the resolution point are free in control-hazard terms; stages added before it cost one bubble each, on every taken branch.

Rule 2 · load-use hazards

A load-use stall lasts as long as the gap between the cycle a consumer needs its operand (its first execute cycle) and the cycle the load's data becomes available. Stages inserted between execute and the point the load data lands widen that gap by one bubble each.

Together these say something useful: the two hazard types pull in opposite directions depending on where you change the depth. That is why "4-stage vs 6-stage" has no single answer, and why the comparison the assignment asks for is a real question rather than a formality.

What it does buy is one fewer forwarding path. In the 5-stage core, three sources feed each operand mux (EX, MA, RW); in 4A there are two (EX, MW). That matters more than it sounds: Schoeberl's Wildcat study found the forwarding multiplexer in the execute stage sits on the critical path, and that widening it as pipelines lengthen can make a 4- or 5-stage core slower than a 3-stage one on the same FPGA. Shallower is not automatically slower.

Pick a pair, and pick it for a reason

Recommended: 4A (IF · OF · EX · MA+RW) against 6G (IF · OF · EX · MA1 · MA2 · RW). They differ in depth by two, they differ in which hazard they pay, and — as the delay analysis below shows — which one wins flips depending on a single parameter. That flip is the finding your report is built on.

If you would rather tell the multiplier story, use 6F instead: RISC201 has mul, div and mod in the ISA, and none of them fit in a single fast ALU cycle. A two-cycle EX is the most defensible engineering reason to go to six stages at all, and it links Part 1a directly to Part 1c.

The delay model — the part you cannot skip

Express every datapath block's delay in FO4 (the delay of an inverter driving four identical inverters) — the standard process-independent unit in this literature. Then the clock period of an N-stage pipeline is the slowest stage plus latch overhead:

T_clk   = max(stage logic delay) + latch overhead
CPI     = 1 + f_br·P_br + f_lu·P_lu + f_muldiv·(L_muldiv − 1)
Time/instruction = CPI × T_clk

Two results fall out immediately, and both are worth writing up.

Splitting a stage that is not the bottleneck buys nothing. In 6F the EX stage is halved to 6 FO4 — and the clock period does not move, because MA is still sitting at 10. You have paid a full bubble on every branch and every load-use for zero frequency. This is Amdahl's law wearing a pipeline costume, and it is the single most common mistake in student pipeline designs.

At this memory latency, the 5-stage reference beats everything. Deeper is not better by default — exactly the conclusion Hartstein and Puzak reach analytically, and Schoeberl reaches experimentally on real silicon.

Sweep the memory latency and the ranking inverts

M is the one parameter you genuinely do not know, so make it the independent variable. In 6G the memory stage is split in two, so its per-stage delay is M/2 — which means once M/2 drops below the EX delay, 6G's clock period stops responding to memory latency entirely.

Lower is faster. Hue encodes depth (petrol = 4-stage, amber = 6-stage); dash encodes the variant. Below roughly M = 9 the shallow cores win; above it, 6G pulls away and never comes back, because it is the only design whose critical stage is no longer memory.

That crossover is your headline result. It says the correct pipeline depth for RISC201 is a function of the memory system, not a property of the ISA — which is precisely the conclusion the pipeline-depth literature has been drawing since 2002, arrived at from your own model.

01b

The microprogrammed control unit

Item 1b asks for a microprogrammed control unit; item 3 asks you to put microcode into the simulator in place of hardwired control. There is a structural catch here that will cost you a week if you notice it late.

Microcode does not drive a pipeline

Classic microprogramming assumes a multi-cycle, bus-based datapath where each microinstruction performs one register-transfer step: MAR ← PC, then MDR ← M[MAR], then IR ← MDR. A pipelined datapath has no such sequencing — every stage does its fixed job every cycle, and "control" is a per-stage signal vector derived combinationally from the opcode. You cannot simply bolt a control store onto your 4-stage core and call it microprogrammed.

So Part 3 is effectively a third execution core: a multi-cycle microcoded machine alongside the two pipelines. Build it that way. (The alternative reading — store each pipeline stage's control vector in a ROM indexed by opcode — is a decode ROM, not microprogramming, and gives you nothing to compare horizontally against vertically.) This is the first thing to raise with your instructor.

The multi-cycle datapath to control

Give the microcoded core its own internal state: PC, IR, MAR, MDR, operand latches A and B, ALUout, FLAGS, plus the 16×32 register file. Every microinstruction asserts a set of control signals for exactly one clock.

The eight LOAD_EN bits are one-hot write enables for A, B, ALUout, MAR, MDR, IR, PC, FLAGS. SEQ selects the next microaddress: 00 fall through, 01 jump to NEXT, 10 dispatch, 11 conditional branch on COND.

The dispatch ROM is the mechanism that matters

Every instruction's microroutine begins with the same fetch sequence and ends by jumping back to it. Between them sits the dispatch: a small 32-entry mapping ROM that turns the 5-bit opcode in IR[31:27] into a microaddress. Get this right and the microprogram is short; get it wrong and you write a 21-way microcoded comparison chain.

; ---- common fetch, 4 microinstructions ----
FETCH:  MAR <- PC                    ; SRC_A=PC, ALU=PASS, LD_MAR
        MDR <- M[MAR]                ; MEM=READ, LD_MDR
        IR  <- MDR ; PC <- PC + 4     ; LD_IR, LD_PC, SRC_B=4, ALU=ADD
        A <- RF[rs1] ; B <- src2       ; LD_A, LD_B, SEQ=DISPATCH
                                        ;   -> uADDR = DISPATCH_ROM[IR[31:27]]

; ---- one routine per opcode ----
U_ADD:  ALUout <- A + B               ; ALU=ADD, LD_ALUOUT
        RF[rd] <- ALUout              ; RF_W=rd, SEQ=JUMP FETCH

U_LD:   ALUout <- A + imm             ; effective address
        MAR <- ALUout
        MDR <- M[MAR]                ; MEM=READ
        RF[rd] <- MDR                 ; SEQ=JUMP FETCH

U_BEQ:  if !flags.E goto FETCH         ; SEQ=CONDBR, COND=NOT_E
        PC <- PC + (offset << 2)     ; SEQ=JUMP FETCH

Horizontal, vertical, nano — and what to measure

The horizontal format above spends one bit (or one small field) per control signal: wide words, maximum parallelism per microinstruction, no decoder. A vertical format encodes the same signals into a compact micro-opcode — say 20 bits — and expands it through a micro-decoder. Narrower control store, but fewer signals assertable per microinstruction, so routines get longer.

Nanoprogramming is the two-level hybrid: a wide vertical micro-store whose entries point into a small nano-store of distinct horizontal control words. It was used in the Nanodata QM-1 and the Motorola 68000, and it pays off precisely when many microinstructions repeat the same control pattern.

01c

The ALU

RISC201 needs add sub mul div mod cmp and or not lsl lsr asr mov plus effective-address arithmetic. "Efficient" here means: pick real algorithms, make them swappable, and report the delay and area each one implies.

Adders — the one place to use published numbers

Parallel prefix adders are a well-characterized family, and the depth/area formulas are exact. This table is the cheapest rigour in the whole project.

Implement at least ripple-carry, Brent–Kung and Kogge–Stone as interchangeable classes. Subtraction reuses the same adder: invert B, set carry-in. That gives sub and cmp for free.

Flags — the correctness trap

cmp sets flags.E and flags.GT. Computing GT as "the subtraction result is positive" is wrong: it breaks on signed overflow. The correct signed condition is

E  = (result == 0)
GT = (N == V) and not Z      # N = result[31], V = signed overflow

Write a directed test for cmp r1, r2 with r1 = 0x7FFFFFFF, r2 = 0x80000000. The naive implementation gets it backwards, and every bgt in every benchmark inherits the bug.

Multiplier

  • Shift-and-add — 32 iterations, one adder. Baseline.
  • Booth radix-2 — same 32 iterations, but handles signed operands natively instead of needing sign-correction.
  • Modified Booth radix-4 — recodes the multiplier two bits at a time: 16 partial products instead of 32.
  • Wallace / Dadda tree — reduces those 16 partial products to two with carry-save adders in about 6 levels, then one 64-bit carry-propagate adder. Radix-4 Booth plus a Wallace tree is the standard high-speed combination.

Divider — and mod comes free

  • Restoring — n iterations; subtract, and restore the partial remainder if it went negative.
  • Non-restoring — n iterations with no restore step; add or subtract based on the sign of the running remainder, with one final correction.
  • SRT radix-2 / radix-4 — redundant quotient digits and a quotient-digit selection table driven by a truncated partial remainder. Worth a line in your report: the 1994 Pentium FDIV bug was five missing entries in exactly such a table.

One divider serves both div and mod — quotient and remainder fall out of the same recurrence. Division by zero raises an exception, which is your first customer for the runtime exception framework in item 2f.

Shifter

A 32-bit barrel shifter is five levels of 2:1 muxes with shift distances 16, 8, 4, 2, 1 — roughly 5 FO4 and, importantly, parallel to the adder rather than in series with it, so it does not extend the ALU critical path. asr fills vacated positions with the sign bit; lsr fills with zero; lsl can share the same network with a reversed input.

This is where the ALU meets Part 1a

mul, div and mod cannot complete in one EX cycle. Whatever you decide here propagates straight back into both pipelines, and you have three options: (i) a multi-cycle iterative unit that stalls the pipeline behind it, (ii) a separate functional unit with its own latency and out-of-order writeback — which introduces WAW hazards you must then handle, or (iii) in the 6-stage, a two-cycle EX (variant 6F) sized to fit a Booth–Wallace multiplier.

Option (iii) is the cleanest justification for a 6-stage RISC201 that exists, and it makes your two cores differ for a reason a hardware engineer would recognise rather than because the assignment asked for two numbers.

Two-pass assembler

Three passes really, because macro expansion must happen before addresses are assigned — if you expand PUSH into two instructions after building the symbol table, every label after it is off by four bytes per push.

  1. Pass 0 · preprocess. Expand stack macros and any user macros. Emit a line map so error messages can point back at the original source.
  2. Pass 1 · symbols. Walk the expanded stream with a location counter, record label → address, reject duplicates. Handle .text/.data directives.
  3. Pass 2 · encode. Emit 32-bit words. Branch offsets are (target − PC) >> 2 in a 27-bit signed field. The 18-bit immediate field is 2 modifier bits plus a 16-bit constant: 00 sign-extend (range −32768…32767), 01 = u zero-extend, 10 = h load to the upper half. Range-check and report the offending line, not an assertion.

Stack macros and the safety net

RISC201 uses a full-descending stack: sp points at the last item pushed, so a push decrements first.

.macro PUSH rX          .macro POP rX
  sub sp, sp, 4         ld  rX, 0[sp]
  st  rX, 0[sp]         add sp, sp, 4
.endm                 .endm

Remember that each PUSH costs two retired instructions. If you count macros as one instruction your CPI numbers will be quietly wrong — count post-expansion.

Item 2f wants runtime memory protection. Check three things on every sp update and every memory access:

  • Alignment — addr & 3 == 0, else MisalignedAccess.
  • Bounds — STACK_LIMIT ≤ addr ≤ STACK_BASE for sp-relative accesses.
  • Guard band — a red zone of a few words below STACK_LIMIT. Any touch is StackOverflow, caught one access before real corruption.

Precise exceptions in a pipeline

When an instruction faults in stage 3 of a 4-stage core, two later instructions are already in flight and one earlier instruction has not retired. Raising the exception immediately corrupts architectural state. The fix: carry a fault code in the pipeline registers alongside the instruction, and only take the exception when that instruction reaches writeback — at which point everything older has retired and everything younger gets squashed. Graders ask about this.

ASCII stack visualizer

Maintain a shadow frame stack: push a record on call (callee label, sp at entry, return address), pop on ret. That is what lets you draw frame boundaries rather than an undifferentiated column of words.

Evaluation and reproducibility

You asked about reproducibility specifically. Here is the protocol that makes item 5 defensible — and that lets you regenerate every number in your report after a late bug fix without redoing the analysis by hand.

Differential testing against a golden model

Write core/functional.py first: a plain single-cycle interpreter with no pipeline, no forwarding, no microcode. It is obviously correct because there is nothing in it to get wrong. Then assert that every other core reaches identical architectural state — all 16 registers, flags, memory, retired instruction count — at every retirement boundary.

Feed it a constrained random program generator: valid encodings only, bounded loop counts, memory accesses confined to a mapped region, no wild jumps. A few thousand random programs will find your forwarding bugs in minutes; hand-written tests will not.

The reproducibility checklist

  • Delay model in a config file, not scattered through the source. config/delays.yaml holds every FO4 figure in §01, with a comment on where each came from. Every reported number is a function of that file.
  • Pinned seeds for the random program generator, committed alongside the failures they found.
  • One command regenerates everything. make repro runs the benchmark matrix, writes a CSV per configuration, and renders every table and figure in your report from those CSVs. No number is ever typed by hand.
  • Committed golden traces so a regression shows up as a diff rather than as a changed summary statistic.
  • Version the ISA table and record its hash in every results file. When the opcode map is corrected, you will know instantly which results predate the fix.
  • Sweep, don't assert. The memory-latency sweep in §01 is one parameter; branch frequency and load-use frequency deserve the same treatment. Report where the crossovers are, not a single point estimate.

Wildcat: Educational RISC-V Microprocessors core

Martin Schoeberl · arXiv:2502.20197 · 2025 · arxiv.org/abs/2502.20197

The most directly relevant paper you will find. Measures 3-, 4- and 5-stage RISC-V cores on FPGA and in a SkyWater130 ASIC flow, and finds — against the textbook — that four and five stages were slower than three, because the execute-stage forwarding multiplexer widens with depth and sits on the critical path. Cite this when you explain why your 4-stage core is competitive.

04

The Optimum Pipeline Depth for a Microprocessor core

Hartstein & Puzak · ISCA 2002 · dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/545214.545217

The analytical theory behind the U-curve your sweep will produce. Shows that greater instruction-level parallelism shortens the optimal pipeline, while fewer stalls lengthen it. Their follow-up (TACO 2004) adds power: optimising for BIPS³/W puts the optimum at about seven stages — a directly quotable anchor for a 4-vs-6 argument.

05

Increasing Processor Performance by Implementing Deeper Pipelines

Sprangle & Carmean, Intel · ISCA 2002 · free PDF

The opposing case, from the Pentium 4 team: deeper pipelines kept paying off, and branch misprediction latency was the single largest contributor to the degradation. Read it alongside Hartstein & Puzak — the disagreement between two ISCA 2002 papers is itself a good paragraph in your report.

06

The Optimal Logic Depth Per Pipeline Stage is 6 to 8 FO4 Inverter Delays

Hrishikesh, Farkas, Jouppi, Burger, Keckler, Shivakumar · ISCA 2002

Where the FO4 methodology in §01 comes from, and the sanity check on your delay budget. Pair with Agarwal, Hrishikesh, Keckler & Burger, "Clock Rate versus IPC" (ISCA 2000) for the wire-delay side of the argument.

07

RV-IM100: Quantifying ISA Extension, Datapath Width and Pipeline Depth Trade-offs

Hyunwoo Kang · arXiv:2605.01902 · 2026 · arxiv.org/abs/2605.01902

Ten FPGA cores from one 5-stage baseline, sweeping depth 5→8. Frequency rose 43→126 MHz, but throughput was non-monotonic — the 6→7 transition regressed absolute throughput despite the higher clock. The closest published analogue of the experiment you are being asked to run, and evidence that a non-monotonic result is a real finding rather than a bug in your simulator.

08

Microprogramming History

Mark Smotherman, Clemson · people.computing.clemson.edu/~mark/uprog.html

The clearest short treatment of horizontal vs vertical encoding and of nanoprogramming, with the real machines that used each — the Nanodata QM-1 (16K×18-bit micro-store over a 1K×360-bit nano-store) and the Motorola 68000. Also makes the point that horizontal microprogramming resembles VLIW scheduling, which is a good framing for your comparison. Wilkes' 1951 paper is the origin if you want to cite it.

09

Computer Arithmetic: Algorithms and Hardware Designs

Behrooz Parhami · and Israel Koren, Computer Arithmetic Algorithms

Either one covers everything in §01c properly: prefix adder taxonomies, Booth recoding, Wallace and Dadda trees, restoring/non-restoring/SRT division. For the adder table specifically, Harris's "A Taxonomy of Parallel Prefix Networks" (Asilomar 2003) is the canonical framework, with Kogge & Stone (1973) and Brent & Kung (1982) as the primary sources.

10

Ripes

Morten Petersen · github.com/mortbopet/Ripes · runs in-browser at ripes.dk

Spend an hour driving it before you design your CLI. It is the most-used teaching simulator for RISC-V and its pipeline view, stage annotations and stall/flush indicators are exactly the UX your pipe command should aim at. WebRISC-V is worth a look too for its cycle-by-cycle table layout.

11

Pedagogically Motivated and Composable Open-Source RISC-V Processors

McDougall et al. · arXiv:2509.20514 · 2025 · arxiv.org/abs/2509.20514

Surveys open RISC-V implementations against criteria for teaching use, then presents a full stack: single-cycle, pipelined and cached reference cores, skeleton versions, verification infrastructure and test generators. Read their verification section — it is a template for the differential-testing protocol in §05. See also basic_RV32s (arXiv:2510.15887) for a documented single-cycle → 5-stage progression.

07

Ten days to the first evaluation

Eval 1 is 30 September. Four or five people, parallelisable if you agree the interfaces on day one. The ordering below front-loads the things everything else depends on.

Days 1–2 · together

  • Confirm the RISC201 opcode map against the handout
  • Write isa/table.py and encoding.py — everyone blocks on this
  • Agree the core interface: step(), state(), stats()
  • Ask about the microcode/pipeline question in §01b

Days 3–5 · split

  • A: assembler passes 0–2 + disassembler + round-trip tests
  • B: golden functional core + memory + exceptions
  • C: ALU classes, adders first, with directed flag tests
  • D: CLI skeleton, load/step/info/x

Days 6–8 · the pipelines

  • 4-stage core, then 6-stage as a parameterised variant
  • Differential test both against the golden core
  • delays.yaml and the stats command
  • Stack macros, guard band, ASCII renderer

Days 9–10 · the result

  • Benchmarks written and running on both cores
  • The memory-latency sweep and its figure
  • make repro end to end
  • Rehearse the demo: pipe and stack are what gets watched

Microcode (items 1b and 3) can trail into the window before the November evaluation — but settle the design question in §01b now, because the answer decides whether it is a third core or a change to an existing one, and that is a week of difference.

Numbers in §01 come from the delay budget stated there, not from measurement — they are a worked example of the method, and your own figures will differ once you set delays.yaml. The value is in stating the assumptions and sweeping the uncertain parameter, which is what makes the 4-vs-6 comparison an argument rather than a table.
